Why Your Business Is Missing From AI Search Results

  Home   : :   Blog  : :  AI Search Optimization

An accountant in Bondi asks a customer how they found the firm. The answer is unexpected: “I asked an AI assistant for a small business accountant near me, and you were one of three it mentioned.” A week later, another owner runs the same query about her own business, in her own suburb, and finds three competitors listed and no sign of her.

It feels arbitrary, and it is unsettling. But the businesses that get named are not paying for it, and have rarely done anything exotic. They have pages an AI can read, understand and safely repeat. That is a smaller and more fixable problem than it first appears.

What has actually changed

Google AI Overviews — the summary that appears above the ordinary blue links — began showing in 2024. Google’s AI Mode, a fuller conversational version of search, started rolling out in Australia in October 2025 as part of an expansion to more than 40 countries. Meanwhile, plenty of people now open ChatGPT or a similar assistant before they open a search box.

The effect on traffic is contested. Australian news publishers have reported year-on-year declines in organic search traffic since AI Overviews launched, with some outlets reporting falls of as much as 35%. Google has said total click volumes from Search remain relatively stable. Neither position is settled.

For a small business, the practical read is simpler. Fewer people browse; more arrive already half-decided, having been told about you by a machine. Being the business the machine mentions is now part of the job.

Why AI systems cannot see you

There are only a handful of common reasons, and none of them is a penalty.

  • Your content is invisible to crawlers. If your text only appears after scripts run, or lives inside images, PDFs or a booking widget, many AI systems will never read it.
  • You block the crawlers. Some site builders and security plugins block AI bots by default. Worth checking what your robots file allows.
  • Your pages do not answer questions. A page headed “Our Approach” that talks about quality and commitment gives an AI nothing to quote. A page that says what you do, where, for whom, and roughly what it costs, gives it plenty.
  • There is no supporting evidence elsewhere. AI answers lean on sources that agree with each other — your site, your Google Business Profile, directories, local media, reviews. One lonely website with no corroboration is a weak citation.
  • You are hard to pin down. No suburb, no service list, no ABN. Machines are cautious about businesses they cannot identify.

What makes a page quotable

Think about what a careful person would need before recommending you to a friend, and put those things in plain text on the page.

  • Ask and answer real questions. Use the customer’s wording as a heading — “How much does a bathroom renovation cost in Sydney?” — then answer it in the first two sentences underneath.
  • Be specific. Suburbs, service names, materials, turnaround times, what is and is not included. Specificity is what an AI can safely repeat; adjectives are not.
  • Front-load the answer. Say the thing, then explain it. Do not build to a conclusion three paragraphs down.
  • Show who is speaking. A named author, real credentials, a licence number where your trade requires one, and a date the page was last updated.
  • Keep claims honest. Australian Consumer Law prohibits misleading or deceptive conduct, and that applies to your website exactly as it applies to a shopfront sign. Inflated claims are also the kind of thing that gets a source treated as unreliable.
  • Use structured data. That is a small block of code, sometimes called schema markup, that states your business details, services and FAQs in a format machines read directly rather than infer.

AI systems do not reward clever writing. They reward pages that make a clear, checkable claim about who you are and what you do.

The Australian specifics worth getting right

Being clearly, verifiably Australian helps an AI place you correctly. A .com.au domain is a decent start: auDA administers the .au namespace, and .com.au requires an Australian presence and a close and substantial connection to the name. Show your ABN, your Sydney suburb, and the areas you cover — Parramatta, Chatswood, Newtown, wherever you actually work.

Consistency across sources matters more than polish. If your site says Surry Hills and your Google Business Profile says Darlinghurst, you have handed the machine a reason to mention someone less ambiguous.

One more thing. If you start feeding customer information into AI tools — enquiry summaries, call transcripts, support chats — the Privacy Act 1988 still governs how you handle personal information. Convenience is not an exemption. Decide early what customer data is allowed near a third-party tool.

Where to start

You do not need a rebuild. Start with an honest look at what a machine can actually read on your site.

  1. Test it. Ask two or three AI assistants for a business like yours in your suburb. Note who gets named and what their pages have that yours does not.
  2. Check readability. View your key page with scripts disabled, or ask whoever maintains your site whether the text is in the page source. If your content only exists inside a widget, that is the first fix.
  3. Rewrite one page properly. Take your most valuable service page. Add three real customer questions as headings. Answer each in two sentences. Add suburbs, inclusions and a last-updated date.
  4. Tidy your details. Same name, address, phone and services everywhere, including your Google Business Profile.

If step two turns up a site that machines simply cannot read, that is a build problem rather than a writing one, and it is what custom web development is for. Common questions

Is AI search replacing Google for small businesses?

Not replacing, but sitting alongside it. Google AI Overviews began appearing above traditional results in 2024, and AI Mode started rolling out in Australia in October 2025. Ordinary search results, maps and your Google Business Profile all still matter.

Should you block AI crawlers from your website?

For most small businesses, no. Blocking them means AI tools cannot read your pages and cannot mention you when someone asks for a supplier. Publishers protecting paid content face a different trade-off to a plumber or an accountant.

Do you need different content for AI search than for Google?

Largely the same content, written more directly. Clear questions with clear answers, specific details, honest pricing signals and plain structure help both a search engine and an AI. Vague marketing language helps neither of them.

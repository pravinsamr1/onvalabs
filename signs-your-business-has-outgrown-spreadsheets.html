Five Signs Your Business Has Outgrown Its Spreadsheets

  Home   : :   Blog  : :  Outgrown Spreadsheets

It is Tuesday afternoon in a warehouse out at Wetherill Park. The operations manager has three files open: the stock sheet, the orders sheet, and the one nobody quite trusts, called Pricing_final_v3_USE THIS.xlsx. A customer is on the phone asking whether their order went out. Answering means asking Sharon, and Sharon is on leave until the ninth.

Nothing here is broken, exactly. The business is busy, the staff are capable, and those spreadsheets did their job faithfully for years. But something has quietly reversed. The files are no longer shaped around the work; the work is now shaped around the files. That shift has warning signs, and they are worth naming before they cost you a customer.

Spreadsheets are not the villain

Let us be fair to Excel and Google Sheets. They are flexible, cheap, and everybody already knows roughly how to use them. Plenty of well-run Australian businesses analyse and budget in spreadsheets and always will. A spreadsheet is a brilliant place to think.

It is a much weaker place to run a process that several people touch, every day, in order. That is where the cracks appear.

The five signs

1. The same information is entered twice

Someone types an order into the sheet, then again into the accounting system, then again into a delivery form. Each retype is another chance for a wrong digit, and none of the three copies knows the others exist.

Duplicate entry is the clearest sign of all, because it is pure cost with no upside. Listen for “I just need to update the other file too.”

2. One person is the only one who understands it

There is a file with clever formulas, hidden columns and a tab called “do not touch”. It works beautifully, and exactly one person knows why.

That is a risk sitting quietly on your books, and in our experience it surfaces at the busiest possible moment. If a process cannot survive one person’s absence, it is not a process. It is a dependency.

3. Nobody is certain which version is current

Version confusion is the most familiar symptom and the easiest to laugh off. The email attachment, the copy on the desktop, the one in the shared drive, the one someone downloaded to work on at home over the weekend.

The problem is not untidiness. It is that two people can make sensible decisions from different numbers on the same afternoon, and neither will know until a customer complains.

4. You cannot tell who changed what, or when

Open a spreadsheet and a figure has changed. Who changed it, when, and why? Usually nobody can say, and the honest answer is a shrug.

This matters beyond curiosity. The ATO has record-keeping expectations for business records, and Fair Work rules set out employee records employers must keep. Where spreadsheets hold personal information about customers or staff, the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles bring obligations too. Get proper advice on your own situation — but a file anyone can quietly edit, copy to a laptop or email as an attachment makes those obligations harder to meet.

5. Reporting takes days, and mistakes survive it

Month-end arrives and someone disappears for two days to assemble a report by copying, pasting and reconciling. By the time it lands, it describes a business that has already moved on.

Worse, errors slide through unnoticed. A row left out of a formula range, a filter nobody cleared, a total that has been wrong since March. Spreadsheets do exactly what they are told and never mention that what they were told looks odd.

If your spreadsheet has become a job rather than a tool, you have already outgrown it. The only question left is which fix is the cheapest one that genuinely works.

The ladder of options, honestly

There are four rungs, and most businesses should start lower than they expect.

Better spreadsheet discipline. Move the file to a shared cloud location so there is one copy with editing history. Restrict who can change formulas. Use data validation to stop nonsense being typed in. Write down how it works. That costs almost nothing and settles version confusion. Try it first.

An off-the-shelf tool. If your need is common — invoicing, rostering, job management, stock, a CRM — someone already sells software for it, and a monthly licence beats building anything. The trap is buying a large platform for one small problem, then bending your business to suit it.

A small custom tool. Sometimes one step is the bottleneck: a job sheet, a quoting calculator, an approval that keeps stalling. A single-purpose tool replacing that one spreadsheet is modest work, and this kind of straightforward software build is where many businesses get the most relief for the least outlay.

A full system. When several processes are tangled together, or you run multiple sites and everyone needs the same picture, the answer may be joined-up custom business software or a broader ERP-style system. It is right sometimes, and oversold often.

What to do next

  1. Pick the one spreadsheet that causes the most trouble. Not all of them. Just the worst one.
  2. Watch it for a fortnight. Note who touches it, what gets retyped elsewhere, and where it stalls. Guesses are cheap; observation is what tells you the real problem.
  3. Try the cheapest rung that could plausibly work. Shared location, locked formulas, validation, a written note on how it works. Surprisingly often, that is enough.
  4. Only then consider building. If the process still fights you after a tidy-up, you now have a clear description of what needs solving — which is what makes a build affordable rather than open-ended.

Common questions

How do we know when it is time to replace a spreadsheet?

When the spreadsheet has become a job rather than a tool. If people spend real time maintaining it, re-entering the same figures, or chasing which copy is current, the file is now creating work instead of saving it. That is the moment to look properly.

Is custom software always more expensive than an off-the-shelf tool?

Usually it costs more upfront, and there is no monthly licence. Off-the-shelf tools are cheaper to start and quicker to adopt, which makes them the right answer for common needs. Custom earns its place when your process genuinely does not match what the market sells.

Can we keep using spreadsheets alongside a new system?

Commonly, yes, and it is often sensible. Spreadsheets remain excellent for analysis, modelling and one-off questions. The aim is to stop them holding the master record of things like orders, stock or staff details, where shared access and history matter.

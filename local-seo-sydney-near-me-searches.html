How to Show Up When Sydney Customers Search "Near Me"

  Home   : :   Blog  : :  Local SEO Sydney

A plumber in Parramatta finishes a job, checks her phone, and finds three new enquiries waiting. Two suburbs over, a competitor with a better van and more experience gets none. The difference is rarely skill. It is that one of them appeared when somebody typed “plumber near me” while standing in a flooded laundry.

That search happens across Sydney thousands of times a day, on a phone, from someone with very little patience. The reassuring part is that most of what decides who appears is admin, not mystery. A lot of it you can sort out yourself this week, without a developer and without spending anything.

What “near me” actually means to Google

Google reads “near me” as “near wherever this person is standing right now”. It swaps in the searcher’s location, then weighs three things:

  • Proximity — how close your business is to the person searching, or whether your listed service area covers them.
  • Relevance — how well you match what they asked for. A “bookkeeper” listing will not surface for “tax agent” unless you have said you do that work.
  • Prominence — how well known and well regarded you appear across the web: reviews, mentions, directory listings, your own site.

Suburb searches like “electrician Chatswood” work the same way, except the person names the location instead of letting their phone supply it. Australians do this constantly, because we think in suburbs, not cities. Someone in Surry Hills searching “physio” will not accept a clinic in Penrith.

You cannot change your proximity. You can change almost everything else.

Your Google Business Profile does most of the heavy lifting

For local searches, your free Google Business Profile often matters more than your website. It fills the map pack — the cluster of three businesses shown with a map above the normal results.

Work through this list carefully:

  • Categories. Pick the most accurate primary category, then add secondary ones. This single field does a great deal of the relevance work.
  • Service area or address. If customers come to you, show the address. If you travel to them, set the suburbs you actually cover.
  • Hours, including public holidays. Wrong hours turn a ready customer into a bad review.
  • Services. List each job in the words customers use, not the words your industry uses.
  • Photos. Real photos of your team, premises and finished work. Stock images read as stock.
  • Reviews. Ask every satisfied customer, and reply to all of them. Note that under Australian Consumer Law, incentivising or fabricating reviews can amount to misleading conduct, so keep it honest.

Then make sure your business name, address and phone number appear identically everywhere online — your site, your Facebook page, directories, invoices. Inconsistent details make you look like two half-businesses rather than one solid one.

Give real pages to your real services and suburbs

Most small business sites have one “Services” page listing everything, plus a footer stuffed with forty suburb names. Search engines have seen that trick for years, and customers find it useless.

A better structure is one page per meaningful service, plus a small number of genuine suburb pages for the areas where you do most of your work. A page about Newtown should mention the jobs you actually do there, the terrace houses and older wiring you meet, parking realities, and a couple of examples. If you cannot write three honest paragraphs about a suburb, you do not need a page for it.

Each page needs the basics: a clear heading saying what you do and where, your phone number visible without scrolling, and one obvious next step. If your site makes new pages painful to add, that is a structural problem worth fixing — exactly what a straightforward website rebuild sorts out permanently.

A page that names the actual job, in the actual suburb, in the words a customer would use, will out-earn a hundred pages that only swap the suburb name.

The quiet technical things that still matter

None of these are glamorous, and all of them help.

Speed on a phone. Google measures three page-experience signals called Core Web Vitals: Largest Contentful Paint, which is good under 2.5 seconds; Interaction to Next Paint, good under 200 milliseconds; and Cumulative Layout Shift, good under 0.1. To pass, at least 75% of real visits to a page must be “good” in Google’s field data. Most slowness comes from oversized images and too many plugins, and decent hosting fixes a surprising amount of the rest.

Structured data. Also called schema markup — a small block of code that spells out your business details for machines, so a search engine can read your address, hours and services without guessing.

An Australian domain. auDA administers the .au namespace, and a .com.au requires an Australian presence and a close, substantial connection to the name. It reads as local, which matters.

Your enquiry form. Collecting names, numbers and addresses means handling personal information, and the Privacy Act 1988 sets expectations for how you do that. A plain privacy page and a form that asks for no more than you need suits most small businesses.

What to do this week

  1. Monday. Claim or log into your Google Business Profile. Fix categories, hours, service area and services. Upload ten real photos.
  2. Tuesday. Search your main service plus your suburb on a phone, logged out. Note who appears above you, and what their listings do differently.
  3. Wednesday. Ask your last ten happy customers for a review. A short, direct message beats a polished campaign.
  4. Thursday. Check your name, address and phone number match across every listing you can find.
  5. Friday. Pick your most profitable service and write it a proper page, naming the suburbs where you do it most.

Common questions

How long does local SEO take to work in Sydney?

It varies with your industry and how competitive your suburb is. Google Business Profile changes can be reflected within days, while website changes and review growth usually build over months. Nobody can promise a position, so be cautious of anyone who does.

Do you need a .com.au domain to rank locally?

No, but it helps Australians recognise you as local. auDA administers the .au namespace, and .com.au requires an Australian presence and a close and substantial connection to the name. That check itself signals you are a genuine local business.

Should you make a page for every Sydney suburb you service?

Only where you genuinely work and can say something specific. A handful of real pages about Parramatta, Chatswood or Bondi, with local detail and relevant jobs, is far more useful than fifty near-identical pages with the suburb name swapped.
